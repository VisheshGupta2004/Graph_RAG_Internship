Concept Block 1

Purpose of the Belt Conveyor System

🧠 Mental Model

The belt conveyor is the continuous coal transport pipeline inside the plant — like the bloodstream of the power plant.

A thermal power plant burns coal to generate steam, which drives a turbine, which spins a generator to produce electricity. The entire generation cycle depends on one simple but critical requirement: a continuous, uninterrupted supply of coal to the boiler.

That is exactly what the belt conveyor system does. It is the internal transportation network that moves coal — hundreds or thousands of tonnes per day — from the point where it is received (the wagon tippler / track hopper) to the coal bunkers located above the boilers. From the bunkers, coal flows down to the pulverisers (mills) that grind it into powder for combustion.

Fig 1.1 – Where the Belt Conveyor System fits in the power generation chain
💡 Why This Matters

If the belt conveyor stops for any reason — mechanical failure, blockage, power trip, misalignment — coal stops moving. Within minutes, the bunker level drops. Within hours, boiler load has to be reduced. If not restored quickly, the entire generating unit may shut down, causing a loss of hundreds of megawatts from the grid. That is the direct consequence of this system failing.

In a typical 500 MW coal-fired unit, the conveyor system must deliver approximately 250–300 tonnes of coal per hour continuously. Any interruption has an immediate, measurable cost — both financial and to the reliability of the power supply.

The belt conveyor is not an isolated machine. It is one critical link in a chain. An imbalance at any point — too much coal, too little coal, wrong size coal — travels downstream and amplifies. A senior engineer thinks of the entire chain simultaneously, not just the conveyor belt in front of them.

Concept Block 3

Conveyor Components & Layout

🧠 Mental Model

A moving belt supported and driven across a structured path — like a moving staircase carrying coal instead of people.

Before you can work safely near a belt conveyor, you must be able to name every component, point to it, and describe its function. On a real plant, supervisors will test this knowledge before allowing you near the equipment. Below is a systematic walkthrough of every major component.

Component-by-Component Breakdown

🔴 Belt

The belt is the actual carrying medium — the surface on which coal rests and moves. It is made of multiple layers: rubber covers on top and bottom, with reinforcing fabric plies (nylon, polyester, or steel cords) in between. The belt forms a continuous loop — the top strand carries coal forward; the bottom (return) strand travels empty back to the start. A typical CHP belt is 800–1400 mm wide and can be hundreds of metres long.

⚠ Hazard

The belt edge is sharp when damaged. The belt surface can drag anything that contacts it. Never stand on or reach over a moving belt.

🔵 Drive Unit (Motor + Gearbox + Drive Pulley)

This is the power source of the conveyor. An electric motor (typically 110–500 kW in a CHP) drives a gearbox that reduces speed and increases torque. The gearbox output shaft is connected to the drive pulley (also called the head pulley). The belt wraps around this pulley, and friction between the pulley surface and the belt drives the entire system. The drive pulley is always at the discharge (head) end.

⚠ Hazard

Pinch point between belt and drive pulley. Extremely high force — instantly fatal if contacted. Must always have a fixed guard.

🟡 Idlers

Idlers are the support rollers that keep the belt in shape and prevent it from sagging under the weight of coal. They are spaced every 1–1.5 metres along the top (carry) strand. Carry idlers are usually mounted in a 3-roll troughing configuration to form a trough shape. Return idlers support the empty belt on the return path and are usually flat or slightly tilted. Idlers rotate freely, reducing friction on the belt.

⚠ Watch Out

Failed (seized) idlers can cause friction, heat, and fire. They also cause belt misalignment. A spinning idler can trap cloth or fingers.

⚪ Tail Pulley

Located at the loading (start) end of the conveyor. The tail pulley is a plain pulley — it does not drive the belt. Its role is simply to act as the return point where the belt loops back. The feed point (where coal drops from the hopper/feeder) is usually just after the tail pulley. The tail pulley is also where belt tension is lowest, making slippage in this region less dangerous — but still a pinch hazard.

🟣 Take-up System

The belt, like any loop under load, must be kept at the correct tension — not too tight, not too loose. If tension is too low, the belt slips on the drive pulley. If too high, bearings and structure are overloaded. The take-up system maintains this tension automatically. In a gravity take-up, a weighted carriage pulls the tail pulley backward using gravity. In a screw take-up, the tail pulley position is adjusted manually. In heavy-duty conveyors, an automatic hydraulic or winch-type take-up is used.

🟤 Feed Point (Loading Zone)

This is where coal is loaded from the hopper or feeder onto the belt. The feed point includes impact idlers (heavier-duty rollers to absorb the impact of falling coal) and skirt boards (rubber or steel side walls that contain the coal on the belt as it is loaded and prevent spillage at the edges). The alignment and height of the drop point critically affects belt loading quality.

⚠ Risk Zone

Spillage is highest at feed points. Wet or fine coal can also block the skirt boards, causing pressure on the belt edge.

🟠 Scrapers & Belt Cleaners

After coal is discharged at the head pulley, a thin layer of fine coal adheres to the belt surface and carries back on the return strand. This is called carryback. Scrapers are blades pressed against the belt surface (at the head pulley and along the return path) to remove this carryback. Without effective scrapers, carryback falls off along the return path, causing spillage, dust, and idler fouling everywhere under the belt.

🔷 Structure, Walkways & Guards

The conveyor is mounted on a steel frame (truss or box structure) that keeps everything aligned and at the correct height. Walkways run alongside for inspection. Nip guards are fitted at all pulley-belt contact points. Emergency pull cords (emergency stop cables) run the full length of the conveyor — pulling the cord anywhere on the conveyor stops it immediately. These are life-saving devices and must never be bypassed.

✅ Remember

If you see a guard removed or a pull cord damaged, report it immediately. These are mandatory safety devices.

Concept Block 4

Functional Mechanism of Conveying

🧠 Mental Model

Friction-driven continuous loop — the motor grips the belt through the drive pulley; the belt carries coal through friction between coal and rubber surface; idlers support the weight.

Understanding how the conveyor actually works — not just what its parts are — is essential to understanding why things go wrong. The entire mechanism depends on three interacting forces: tension, friction, and gravity.

Fig 4.1 – Drive mechanism schematic: Power path from motor to belt

The Three Pillars of Conveyor Motion

Force / PrincipleWhat It DoesIf Disturbed
Friction (Drive)The motor turns the drive pulley; the rubber surface of the pulley grips the belt through friction and pulls it forward. Higher friction coating (lagging) on the pulley increases grip.If coal, water, or grease gets on the pulley surface, friction is lost → belt slips → coal stops moving → pulley overheats.
Belt TensionThe take-up system maintains a minimum tension in the belt so the belt wraps tightly around the drive pulley. This ensures the friction force can be transmitted effectively.Too low tension → belt slips on pulley. Too high → bearing overload, structure damage, belt stretch.
Idler SupportIdlers distribute the weight of the coal-loaded belt across the structure, reducing the effective force the motor must overcome. They also maintain belt shape (troughing).Failed idler → belt sags → uneven load → increased friction → motor overload → fire risk.
📐 Engineering Note – Tight Side vs Slack Side

The belt tension is not equal everywhere. On the tight side (belt leaving the drive pulley on the carry strand), tension is high — the belt is being pulled. On the slack side (belt returning to the drive pulley), tension is lower. The difference between these two tensions is what propels the belt and coal forward. This concept explains why adequate take-up tension is so critical — insufficient slack-side tension means the drive pulley cannot transmit enough force.

Why the Belt Drifts (Misalignment)

The belt naturally tends to drift (wander sideways) if there is any asymmetry — uneven loading, idlers not perfectly perpendicular to belt travel, wind, or a bent frame. Even a slight drift accumulates over the length of the belt. If not corrected, the belt edge contacts the conveyor structure, causing edge damage, spillage, and ultimately belt failure. Self-aligning idlers and training idlers are used to correct drift automatically.

🔴 Critical Consequence

A drifted belt that contacts the steel structure can generate enough heat through friction to ignite coal dust — causing a conveyor fire. Belt fires in CHP are a major risk and have caused plant outages lasting weeks.

Concept Block 5

Conveyor Capacity & Throughput

🧠 Mental Model

Think of capacity as a "pipe size" — the pipe can carry only so much per second. Push more in than it can carry, and it overflows. Push less, and the plant is underfed.

Every belt conveyor is designed to carry a specific maximum tonnage per hour (TPH). This is called the rated capacity. The conveyor design — belt width, speed, inclination, and idler spacing — is optimised for this rated capacity. Operating at or above this limit causes problems; operating well below causes inefficiency.

⚠ Overloading – What Happens

When a conveyor is overloaded: (1) coal spills over the belt edges; (2) the motor draws excess current and may trip on overload protection; (3) the belt sags between idlers, increasing friction; (4) the drive may slip; (5) structural stress on the frame increases. Repeated overloading shortens belt life significantly.

⚠ Underloading – What Happens

Underloading (running a conveyor almost empty) wastes electrical energy and accelerates belt wear due to the constant flexing of an unloaded belt over idlers. It also reduces the effective cleaning action of belt cleaners and increases dust generation from the empty belt vibrating on return idlers.

💡 Capacity in Context

A 500 MW thermal unit needs approximately 250–300 TPH of coal at full load. If the belt conveyor can carry 400 TPH rated capacity, there is a buffer. If an upstream crusher or feeder fails and reduces supply to 150 TPH, the bunker level gradually falls and load must be reduced. An intern does not control these rates — but understanding this relationship lets you understand the urgency when a supervisor responds to a falling bunker level.

Concept Block 6

Transfer Points & Flow Behaviour

🧠 Mental Model

A transfer point is like a controlled handover baton pass — coal must land precisely on the next belt. Any miss, blockage, or misalignment at this point disrupts the entire downstream flow.

Wherever coal moves from one conveyor to the next — or from a hopper to a conveyor — there is a transfer point. These are not minor connections; they are among the most critical and most hazardous locations in the entire Coal Handling Plant. Understanding what happens at a transfer point is essential before entering the CHP.

🔴 Safety – Never Enter a Transfer Tower Alone

Transfer towers are enclosed structures at junctions between conveyors. They are high-dust, confined-space environments with active machinery on multiple levels. Entry requires a Confined Space Entry Permit, dust mask, and must always be done with a buddy. Never enter during conveyor operation without a specific maintenance permit.

Concept Block 7

Safety & Hazard Awareness

🧠 Mental Model

Moving machinery + bulk material + confined spaces = continuous risk. Most conveyor accidents happen not because of complexity but because someone was not aware of where the danger was.

🔴 Senior Engineer's Warning to Every New Trainee

You do not need to be doing anything wrong to be injured near a conveyor. You simply need to be in the wrong place, distracted, or unaware of what is moving around you. The conveyor does not slow down for you. It does not have a sensor that detects a human body near it. It will continue running at full speed regardless. Your awareness is your protection.

Hazard-by-Hazard Analysis

🔴 Hazard 1 – Rotating Parts (Pulleys & Idlers)

Pulleys rotate continuously at high speed. The nip point — where the belt meets the pulley — has an extremely small gap that closes in milliseconds. A finger, loose clothing, or hair entering this gap will be drawn in with full motor force before any human reaction is possible. Guards (physical covers) must always be in place over all pulleys. If a guard is missing — report it immediately and do not approach the pulley.

🔴 Hazard 2 – Moving Belt (Entanglement)

The belt surface itself is a hazard. Loose clothing, gloves, tools, or anything trailing near the belt can be instantly grabbed and pulled in. Belt speed (1.5–2.5 m/s) is faster than human reflex. This type of accident typically results in degloving injuries, crush injuries, or fatalities. Rule: always wear close-fitting PPE near conveyors. Never lean over a running belt.

🟠 Hazard 3 – Falling Coal (Impact Hazard)

At transfer points, coal falls from one conveyor to the next. Large coal lumps weighing several kilograms can bounce or be thrown sideways if a blockage or imbalance occurs. Standing below or beside an active chute is extremely dangerous. Transfer towers also have open floors and structural walkways at multiple levels — falling coal from one level can injure personnel on a lower level.

🟠 Hazard 4 – Coal Dust (Respiratory & Fire)

Fine coal dust generated at transfer points, feed points, and from carryback is a dual hazard. Respiratory: Coal dust causes coal worker's pneumoconiosis (CWP/"black lung") with long-term exposure. Always wear a P2/P3 dust mask in dusty areas. Fire: Coal dust suspended in air at concentrations above the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL) can ignite from a spark or hot surface. Accumulated coal dust on hot surfaces (like a seized idler or a slipping belt) can also self-ignite.

🟠 Hazard 5 – Slips, Trips & Falls

Coal spillage on walkways creates an extremely slippery surface, especially when wet. Carryback coal falls from the return strand onto the floor below the conveyor. Structural platforms, stairs, and walkways in CHP are often narrow and positioned close to moving equipment. Falls from elevated walkways (transfer towers can be 10–20 m high) are fatal. Always hold handrails, wear non-slip safety boots, and never rush on CHP walkways.

🔴 Hazard 6 – Electrical Hazards

Conveyor drive panels operate at medium voltage (3.3 kV or 6.6 kV in large plants, 415 V in smaller). Control panels, junction boxes, and local isolators are located along conveyor routes. Never open an electrical panel without a qualified electrician present and without following Lockout-Tagout (LOTO) procedures. Water from coal spillage near electrical panels is extremely dangerous.

✅ Emergency Pull Cord – Never Ignore

A red-coloured rope or wire runs the full length of every conveyor, both sides. This is the Emergency Pull Cord (EPC). Pulling it anywhere along the conveyor immediately stops the conveyor via the Emergency Stop relay. If you see an unsafe condition, an entanglement, or any emergency — pull it. Do not hesitate. A conveyor trip can be restarted after investigation. A human injury cannot be undone. You will never be penalised for pulling the emergency cord in a genuine emergency.

Concept Block 8

Types of Wagon Tippler – Upstream Context

🧠 Mental Model

The wagon tippler is the starting gun — the moment coal enters the plant. Different tippler types deliver coal at different rates, lump sizes, and patterns, each with a different impact on the first belt conveyor.

Coal arrives at a thermal power plant in railway wagons — each carrying 60–80 tonnes of coal. The wagon tippler is the mechanism that unloads these wagons. The type of tippler directly affects:

🔗 How Tippler Type Affects Your Conveyor

When a rotary tippler unloads a wagon, coal arrives at the hopper in a surge — a large volume in a short time — followed by a pause as the next wagon is positioned. This surge must be absorbed by the hopper before it reaches the feeder and then the belt. If the hopper is small or the feeder is not controlled properly, the surge can overload the belt. Conversely, during the changeover between wagons, the belt may run empty. This cyclic loading is normal for rotary tippler plants, and belt systems are designed to handle it — but a new intern should understand why the loading on the belt appears uneven.

Control Points

Critical Process Control Points

Control points are the locations in the system where the quality, continuity, and safety of coal flow are most sensitive. These are the points a plant engineer monitors most carefully. As an intern, you will hear these terms constantly — understanding what they mean is essential.

CP-1

Coal Loading onto Belt (Feed Point)

Where: Just after the tail pulley, where coal from the hopper/feeder drops onto the moving belt.

What is critical here: The coal must be deposited centrally on the belt, at a controlled rate (not exceeding rated TPH), and with minimum free-fall height to reduce impact and dust. Skirt boards must form a tight seal at the edges to contain coal. Any gap in the skirt board → immediate spillage.

⚠ Risk

Overloading at CP-1 causes belt sag, edge spillage, and motor overload. Blocked skirt boards cause belt edge damage. Impact from large coal lumps at feed point damages belt cover.

CP-2

Transfer Between Conveyors (Transfer Point)

Where: Transfer tower — where coal discharges from BC-1 and loads onto BC-2 through a chute.

What is critical here: The trajectory of coal through the chute must be matched to the receiving belt speed and direction. The coal must land centrally on BC-2. The chute must be free of blockage. Chute liners (wear-resistant steel plates) must be intact. Deflector plates must direct coal accurately.

⚠ Risk

This is the highest-risk location in the entire CHP. Blockages here can back-fill the chute, causing BC-1 overload. Spillage from here falls from height. Dust is at maximum. Confined space entry here requires a formal permit.

CP-3

Belt Movement at Drive Pulley

Where: Head end of conveyor — drive pulley, motor, gearbox assembly.

What is critical here: The drive pulley must maintain sufficient friction with the belt to transmit motor torque without slipping. The belt must wrap around the pulley at the correct arc (wrap angle). The head scraper must effectively remove coal from the belt surface as it discharges. The belt must discharge coal cleanly into the chute below.

⚠ Risk

Extreme mechanical hazard zone. The nip point here has the highest torque in the system. Guard must always be in place. Belt slip here causes overheating and fire. Scraper failure leads to carryback and belt underside fouling.

CP-4

Belt Alignment Along Structure

Where: Monitored continuously along the full length of the conveyor using belt sway switches.

What is critical here: The belt must remain centred on the idlers throughout its full length. Belt sway switches are installed at regular intervals — if the belt drifts beyond a set limit, the sway switch first gives an alarm; if drift continues, it trips the conveyor. Self-aligning (training) idlers automatically correct minor drift. Regular inspection of idler alignment and condition is essential to maintaining belt tracking.

⚠ Risk

Belt drift → edge contact with structure → friction heat → fire risk. Spillage along the full belt length. Edge damage requires expensive belt replacement.

CP-5

Discharge at Coal Bunker

Where: Head end of the final conveyor — above the coal bunker.

What is critical here: Coal must discharge into the correct bunker bay. In large plants, a tripper car (a movable discharge device on the belt) distributes coal across multiple bunker bays to maintain even filling. Bunker level indicators monitor how full each bay is. If a bunker overflows, coal falls into the boiler bay — a serious fire and housekeeping hazard. If a bunker starves, the boiler load must be reduced immediately.

⚠ Risk

Bunker overflow → coal on boiler bay floor → fire risk near hot surfaces. Bunker starvation → forced load reduction → revenue loss. Dust in bunker bay is particularly hazardous — bunker bays are semi-enclosed high-dust zones.

Risk of Failure

Consequences When the System Fails

Understanding failure consequences is not pessimism — it is the foundation of safety and operational discipline. Every component has a reason it exists, and that reason becomes clear the moment it fails. Below is a structured view of what happens when the belt conveyor system fails, across three dimensions.

🏭 Plant Continuity

  • Conveyor stoppage → coal supply interrupted → bunker level falls within hours
  • Bunker starvation → boiler load must be reduced → generation loss (MW reduction)
  • Prolonged stoppage → boiler trips → complete unit shutdown
  • Flow imbalance (feeder too fast) → belt overload → conveyor trips → same outcome
  • Downstream impact on grid: power deficit, frequency instability
  • Estimated loss per hour of outage for a 500 MW unit: ₹15–25 lakhs (at current rates)

⚙ Machinery Integrity

  • Belt damage from overload or misalignment → longitudinal rip → belt replacement (₹5–50 lakhs per belt)
  • Idler seizure → local friction → belt fire → possible total belt loss
  • Drive pulley slip → overheating of lagging → rubber deterioration → reduced friction → more slip
  • Gearbox overload from blocked conveyor → gear damage → weeks of downtime
  • Take-up failure → belt slack → catastrophic slippage and pile-up at head end
  • Transfer chute wear → liner failure → chute structural damage → extended shutdown

👷 Human Safety

  • Entanglement in moving belt → degloving, amputation, fatality
  • Entanglement in drive pulley nip → instant, irreversible crush injury
  • Falling coal at transfer point → blunt trauma, fracture
  • Belt fire → smoke inhalation, burn injuries
  • Coal dust explosion → blast and burn injuries across large area
  • Slipping on coal spillage → fall from height in transfer towers (potentially fatal)
  • Electrical contact near damaged panels → electrocution
🔴 The Senior Engineer's Perspective on Risk

Every failure in this list was, at some point, preventable. Most machinery failures begin with a small defect — a seized idler, a worn scraper, a loose guard, a misaligned belt — that was visible for days before the failure happened. The difference between a major accident and a near-miss is often one person who was paying attention and reported what they saw. As an intern, your most valuable contribution to plant safety is having the awareness to recognise and report early signs of deterioration — and this course is giving you exactly that awareness.

Failure Cases

Real Failure Scenarios – Cause & Effect Analysis

The following failure scenarios are based on common real-world incidents in Coal Handling Plants across India and globally. Study each one carefully — during your site visit, you should be able to recognise the early warning signs of each.

1

Uneven Loading → Belt Drift → Structural Damage

Cause: Coal from the feeder drops consistently to one side of the belt (e.g., feeder gate partially blocked, chute misaligned). The belt carries more load on one side than the other.

What Happens: The belt gradually drifts toward the heavier side. It contacts the steel frame of the conveyor structure. Friction between the belt edge and the frame generates heat. Over hours, the belt edge begins to abrade. Coal spillage begins at the affected side. If not corrected, the belt tears longitudinally — a full-length split requiring complete belt replacement.

Early Signs: Belt sway alarm activates. Visible drift at one or more idler sets. Spillage on one side only. Squealing noise from belt-structure contact.

Consequence: Forced conveyor shutdown. Belt replacement cost (₹10–40 lakhs). 2–5 days of downtime for re-belting.

2

Overloading → Belt Stress → Spillage → Motor Trip

Cause: The feeder speed is set too high, or a sudden surge from the wagon tippler overwhelms the hopper. Coal falls on the belt at a rate exceeding rated capacity.

What Happens: Coal piles up on the belt, exceeding the trough capacity. Spillage occurs from both edges. The belt sags more between idlers, increasing rolling resistance. Motor current rises. Motor protection relay detects overload and trips the motor. The belt stops. Coal continues to fall from the hopper onto the stationary belt, burying it under a pile of coal.

Early Signs: High motor current alarm. Increased spillage at feed point. Belt visibly overloaded (coal reaching edge of trough). Vibration increase.

Consequence: Conveyor buried under coal. Manual digging-out required (hours of labour). Belt may be damaged under the weight. Entire coal flow stopped.

3

Poor Transfer Point Flow → Chute Blockage → System Shutdown

Cause: Wet, fine coal (after rain or due to high moisture content) builds up on the inner walls of the transfer chute. Over time, coal bridges across the chute opening.

What Happens: Coal flow through the chute reduces, then stops. BC-1 continues running — coal piles up at the head pulley discharge point and overflows the chute. BC-2 runs empty, drawing coal from the bunker without being replenished. Bunker level falls. Eventually, BC-1 is tripped on overload as coal piles up. The plant is called to reduce load.

Early Signs: BC-2 belt running empty (visible drop in load current). Spillage at base of transfer chute. Unusual noise from chute (hollow sound instead of coal flow sound).

Consequence: Manual chute cleaning required. Confined space permit needed. Risk of coal avalanche when blockage suddenly clears.

4

Belt Slipping on Drive Pulley → Loss of Conveying → Overheating

Cause: Water or coal slurry contaminates the surface of the drive pulley, reducing friction. Or the take-up tension is insufficient. The belt cannot maintain grip on the rotating pulley.

What Happens: The drive pulley continues to rotate, but the belt slips — moving slower than the pulley surface. This generates significant heat at the pulley-belt contact zone. The rubber lagging on the pulley begins to burn. Acrid smoke is generated. If the conveyor is not stopped, the lagging is destroyed, and eventually the belt itself can ignite. Meanwhile, coal stops moving because the belt is stationary or barely moving.

Early Signs: Burning rubber smell. Smoke from head end. Reduced belt speed indicator. High motor current (motor is working hard but belt is not moving at designed speed).

Consequence: Belt fire — major safety incident. Drive pulley lagging replacement. Potential partial belt damage. 1–3 days outage.

5

Dust Accumulation Ignition → Conveyor Fire

Cause: Coal dust accumulates on conveyor structure, idlers, and the underside of the belt over weeks without adequate housekeeping. A seized idler generates frictional heat. This heat ignites the accumulated coal dust.

What Happens: Smouldering begins on the structure or inside the belt tunnel. Because coal dust is distributed over a large area, the fire can spread rapidly along the full length of the conveyor. In an enclosed conveyor (gallery), fire and smoke can develop quickly. A conveyor fire can destroy the entire belt (hundreds of metres long), the structure, and adjacent equipment.

Early Signs: Burning smell. Smoke in the conveyor gallery. Seized idler (idler not rotating despite belt moving — creates a flat worn spot on the idler and local heating). Unusual hotspot detected by thermal camera.

Consequence: Major fire incident. Full belt replacement. Structural repairs. Plant outage of weeks. Regulatory investigation. Possible fatalities if personnel are in the gallery.

6

Exposure to Moving Parts → Entanglement Injury

Cause: A person reaches over or near a running conveyor without adequate clearance. Loose clothing, gloves, or hair contacts the moving belt or a rotating idler/pulley.

What Happens: The belt instantly grips the material and draws the person's limb or clothing into the nip point. At 1.5 m/s belt speed and with a motor delivering hundreds of kilowatts, the force is thousands of times greater than human strength. No one can pull free. The injury is instantaneous and typically irreversible — degloving, crush, amputation, or fatal entrapment.

Prevention: Guard all nip points (mandatory, not optional). Never approach a running conveyor without proper authorisation. Emergency pull cord must be functional and tested. Buddy system — never work alone near running equipment.

Consequence: Serious injury or fatality. Plant shutdown for investigation. Management liability. Irreparable human loss.

🔴 Zero Tolerance

There is zero tolerance in a professional plant for bypassing guards, reaching over running conveyors, or working near running equipment without proper PPE and procedures. This is not bureaucracy — it is the direct lesson from real fatalities.

Section A – Multiple Choice (20 Questions)
Q1 · Purpose of Conveyor System
What is the primary function of the belt conveyor system in a thermal power plant?
  • A) To generate electricity from coal
  • B) To continuously transport coal from the unloading area to the coal bunkers
  • C) To crush coal into powder before it enters the boiler
  • D) To store coal during peak demand periods
✅ Correct AnswerB) The belt conveyor's primary function is continuous transportation of coal from the receiving point (wagon tippler/hopper) to the coal bunkers. It does not generate electricity, crush coal, or provide primary storage.
Q2 · System Flow
In the correct sequence of coal flow, which stage comes immediately AFTER the wagon tippler and BEFORE the belt conveyor?
  • A) Boiler
  • B) Coal Bunker
  • C) Track Hopper / Feeder
  • D) Transfer Tower
✅ Correct AnswerC) After the wagon tippler, coal falls into the track hopper (which buffers it), and then a feeder controls the rate at which coal is loaded onto the conveyor belt. The boiler and bunker are downstream, and the transfer tower is between conveyors.
Q3 · Conveyor Components
Which component is responsible for maintaining the correct tension in the belt at all times?
  • A) Head Pulley
  • B) Carry Idler
  • C) Take-up System
  • D) Belt Scraper
✅ Correct AnswerC) The take-up system (gravity or screw type) maintains belt tension. The head pulley drives the belt. Idlers support the belt. Scrapers clean the belt surface after discharge.
Q4 · Drive Mechanism
How is the belt actually moved forward by the motor?
  • A) A chain attached from the motor to the belt pulls it directly
  • B) The motor pumps hydraulic oil that moves the belt through pistons
  • C) Friction between the rotating drive pulley surface and the belt drives the belt forward
  • D) Magnetic attraction between the belt and the motorised frame pulls the belt
✅ Correct AnswerC) The belt conveyor is a friction-drive system. The motor rotates the drive pulley, and the friction between the pulley surface (usually rubber-lagged) and the belt causes the belt to move. This is why belt slippage occurs when there is insufficient friction or tension.
Q5 · Idler Function
What is the purpose of carry idlers being arranged in a troughed (angled) configuration rather than flat?
  • A) To reduce the speed of the belt for safety
  • B) To increase the cross-sectional area of coal on the belt, increasing carrying capacity
  • C) To make the belt easier to clean
  • D) To allow the belt to change direction
✅ Correct AnswerB) Troughing the belt by angling the side rollers creates a concave shape that holds significantly more coal per metre of belt than a flat configuration — dramatically increasing throughput capacity at the same belt speed.
Q6 · Belt Capacity
A conveyor is rated at 350 TPH. If the feeder upstream is delivering 480 TPH, what will MOST LIKELY happen?
  • A) The conveyor automatically adjusts its speed to handle the extra load
  • B) The coal will be stored in the hopper until capacity is available
  • C) The belt will be overloaded — causing spillage, high motor current, and possible conveyor trip
  • D) The extra coal will be automatically diverted to a secondary conveyor
✅ Correct AnswerC) Exceeding rated capacity causes overloading — coal spills off the belt edges, the motor draws high current (overload condition), and the conveyor protection system may trip the motor. Conveyors do not automatically adjust speed in response to overloading.
Q7 · Transfer Points
Which of the following is the MOST serious risk specific to transfer points in a Coal Handling Plant?
  • A) Belt running at high speed
  • B) Combination of chute blockage, coal dust, confined space, and falling coal
  • C) The head pulley getting too hot
  • D) Insufficient belt tension at the transfer location
✅ Correct AnswerB) Transfer points concentrate multiple hazards simultaneously: confined space (permit required), maximum dust generation, risk of chute blockage causing coal avalanche, and falling coal from height. This is why transfer towers are the highest-risk location in CHP.
Q8 · Safety Devices
What is the immediate action of the Emergency Pull Cord (EPC) when activated?
  • A) It sounds an alarm to alert the control room
  • B) It slows the conveyor gradually to a stop over 30 seconds
  • C) It immediately stops the conveyor via an emergency stop relay
  • D) It activates the fire suppression system
✅ Correct AnswerC) The EPC triggers an emergency stop relay that immediately cuts power to the drive motor, stopping the conveyor as quickly as possible (within belt coasting distance). It does not merely alarm — it stops the conveyor. This is a life-safety device.
Q9 · Hazard Awareness
You notice an idler roller is not rotating even though the belt is moving over it. What is the CORRECT action?
  • A) Manually kick the idler to get it spinning again
  • B) Ignore it — idlers often stop temporarily and restart on their own
  • C) Report it immediately to the supervisor — a seized idler generates friction heat and is a fire risk
  • D) Pour water on it to cool it down
✅ Correct AnswerC) A seized idler is a critical early warning sign. The stationary idler creates a friction hotspot where the belt slides over it — generating heat that can ignite coal dust. Immediate reporting enables planned maintenance before a fire occurs. Never touch or manually interact with running conveyor components.
Q10 · Wagon Tippler
Which type of wagon unloading system produces the most CONTINUOUS and EVEN coal feed to the conveyor?
  • A) Rotary Tippler
  • B) Side Discharge Tippler
  • C) Track Hopper System
  • D) All three produce identical feed patterns
✅ Correct AnswerC) The Track Hopper System, where bottom-discharge wagons continuously drop coal through grated tracks as the wagon moves, provides the most continuous feed. Rotary and side discharge tippers operate in batches (one wagon at a time), creating surge-and-pause loading patterns.
Q11 · Belt Mechanism
What happens to the belt conveyor system if belt tension drops below the minimum required value?
  • A) The motor speed increases automatically to compensate
  • B) The belt slips on the drive pulley — causing loss of conveying and possible overheating
  • C) The belt becomes tighter due to coal weight
  • D) Nothing — tension does not affect conveyor performance
✅ Correct AnswerB) Insufficient belt tension reduces the normal force pressing the belt against the drive pulley, reducing the available friction force. The drive pulley then spins but the belt slides (slips) — stopping coal movement, generating heat at the pulley-belt interface, and potentially causing fire.
Q12 · Safety PPE
Which item of PPE should be REMOVED (not worn) when working near rotating parts like pulleys and idlers?
  • A) Safety helmet
  • B) Safety boots
  • C) Loose-fitting gloves
  • D) High-visibility vest
✅ Correct AnswerC) Loose gloves near rotating parts are a serious entanglement hazard. The glove can be grabbed by a rotating idler or belt edge and draw the hand and arm into the rotating machinery. If gloves must be worn for hand protection, they must be close-fitting and must not be worn near running rotating parts.
Q13 · Failure Consequences
If the belt conveyor to the coal bunker stops for 4 hours and no alternative coal supply is available, what is the DIRECT consequence to the power plant?
  • A) The boiler automatically switches to an alternative fuel
  • B) The plant continues at full load using coal stored in the bunker only
  • C) Bunker level falls progressively — boiler load must be reduced and the unit may shut down
  • D) The plant shuts down immediately when the conveyor stops
✅ Correct AnswerC) The bunker provides a buffer (typically 8–12 hours at full load). A 4-hour stoppage will significantly reduce bunker levels. Load must be reduced as bunker level falls. If the bunker empties completely, the unit shuts down. The plant does not immediately shut down — but the countdown begins the moment the conveyor stops.
Q14 · Belt Drift
What is the PRIMARY consequence of belt drift if it is not corrected in time?
  • A) Increased coal capacity due to wider tracking
  • B) Belt edge contacts steel structure → friction → heat → fire risk and belt damage
  • C) Reduced motor current because of lower friction
  • D) Automatically activates the emergency pull cord
✅ Correct AnswerB) Belt drift causes the edge of the belt to rub against the steel conveyor structure. The friction generates heat, which can ignite accumulated coal dust, causing a fire. The belt edge also gets progressively damaged, leading to belt failure. Belt sway switches give an alarm first, then trip the conveyor if drift continues uncorrected.
Q15 · Transfer Chute
What is the function of the transfer chute at a transfer point?
  • A) To store excess coal between conveyors
  • B) To guide coal discharged from one conveyor and direct it centrally onto the next conveyor
  • C) To crush large coal lumps before they reach the next conveyor
  • D) To measure the weight of coal passing between conveyors
✅ Correct AnswerB) The transfer chute's sole function is to guide coal from the discharge point of one conveyor and ensure it lands centrally on the next conveyor belt. It must be shaped, angled, and maintained so that coal flows smoothly without blockage, spillage, or off-centre landing.
Q16 · Coal Dust Hazard
Coal dust poses TWO distinct safety hazards. Which answer correctly identifies BOTH?
  • A) Electrical short circuit and structural corrosion
  • B) Respiratory disease (black lung / CWP) AND fire/explosion risk
  • C) Slipping hazard only when wet
  • D) Accelerated belt wear and motor overheating only
✅ Correct AnswerB) Coal dust is a dual hazard: (1) Long-term inhalation causes Coal Workers' Pneumoconiosis (CWP / "black lung") — a serious, irreversible lung disease; (2) Coal dust suspended in air at sufficient concentration can ignite and cause a dust explosion or fire. Both hazards require appropriate controls (dust suppression, PPE, housekeeping).
Q17 · Rotary Tippler
In a plant using a rotary tippler, why does the coal loading on the belt conveyor appear uneven — with periods of heavy load followed by reduced load?
  • A) Because the belt speed varies automatically
  • B) Because coal is unloaded in batches — one wagon at a time — with a pause between wagons during changeover
  • C) Because the feeder has two speeds
  • D) Because the coal bunker level fluctuates
✅ Correct AnswerB) The rotary tippler unloads wagons one at a time. While one wagon is being tipped, coal surges into the hopper. During the changeover (removing the empty wagon and positioning the next loaded wagon), no coal enters the hopper. This creates a cyclic surge-pause pattern on the belt. The hopper and feeder are designed to buffer this, but the pattern still influences belt loading.
Q18 · Component Function
What is the role of the belt scraper (head scraper) positioned at the drive/head pulley?
  • A) To apply tension to the belt
  • B) To remove fine coal (carryback) adhering to the belt surface after discharge, preventing it from falling along the return path
  • C) To slow the belt down before the head pulley
  • D) To guide the belt onto the drive pulley correctly
✅ Correct AnswerB) Fine coal sticks to the belt surface even after the coal load has been discharged at the head end. Without the scraper, this "carryback" falls off along the return path — creating spillage, dust, and fouled return idlers everywhere under the belt. The scraper blade presses against the belt surface at the head pulley to remove this layer.
Q19 · Failure Mechanism
A conveyor fire starts due to a seized idler. Which sequence correctly describes how this develops?
  • A) Seized idler → belt stretches → motor trips → fire starts at motor
  • B) Seized idler → belt slides over stationary idler → friction heat generated → coal dust ignites → fire spreads
  • C) Seized idler → belt drifts → coal spills → spontaneous combustion
  • D) Seized idler → take-up fails → belt goes slack → fire from pulley slip
✅ Correct AnswerB) The correct mechanism is: seized idler (not rotating) → moving belt slides over the stationary idler surface creating friction → heat builds up at that local point → accumulated coal dust on and around the idler ignites from the heat → fire spreads along the belt and structure. This is why regular idler inspection and immediate reporting of seized idlers is so important.
Q20 · Capacity Factors
If a conveyor's belt speed is doubled (all other factors unchanged), what happens to the conveyor's throughput capacity?
  • A) Capacity stays the same — only the belt wears faster
  • B) Capacity approximately doubles — more tonnes move per hour at double the speed
  • C) Capacity reduces — higher speed reduces trough depth
  • D) Capacity increases by 50% due to vibration losses at high speed
✅ Correct AnswerB) Since capacity = cross-sectional area × belt speed × bulk density, doubling the belt speed (with the same cross-sectional load) approximately doubles the TPH throughput. However, practical limits exist — higher speed increases dust generation, impact at feed points, belt wear, and noise. Engineers balance speed with these practical constraints.
Section B – True or False (10 Questions)
Q21 · True / False
The tail pulley is connected to the motor and is responsible for driving the belt forward.
✅ Answer: FALSEThe tail pulley is NOT connected to the motor. It is a free-rotating (passive) pulley at the loading end of the conveyor. The drive (head) pulley at the discharge end is connected to the motor via the gearbox and is responsible for driving the belt.
Q22 · True / False
In a thermal power plant, a belt conveyor stoppage immediately shuts down the entire power generating unit.
✅ Answer: FALSEThe coal bunker provides a buffer (typically 8–12 hours at full load). A conveyor stoppage does not cause immediate shutdown — but it starts a countdown. Load must be progressively reduced as the bunker level falls. Only if the bunker empties completely will the boiler be forced to shut down. This is why quick restoration of the conveyor is so urgent.
Q23 · True / False
The troughed idler configuration increases the carrying capacity of the belt compared to a flat idler arrangement.
✅ Answer: TRUEBy angling the side idler rolls (typically 20–45°), the belt forms a trough shape, significantly increasing the cross-sectional area of coal it can carry. This is why virtually all bulk material conveyors use troughed idlers rather than flat idlers.
Q24 · True / False
Entering a transfer tower during conveyor operation is permissible as long as you wear a dust mask and helmet.
✅ Answer: FALSETransfer towers are classified as confined spaces with active machinery. Entry during operation requires a Confined Space Entry Permit (CSEP) regardless of PPE worn. PPE alone does not make entry safe — the permit system ensures isolation of energy sources, gas testing, buddy system, rescue plan, and supervisor sign-off. Entering without a permit is a serious safety violation.
Q25 · True / False
The rotary tippler requires specially designed bottom-discharge railway wagons.
✅ Answer: FALSEThe rotary tippler works with standard BOX-N type wagons (the most common coal wagon in India). It physically rotates the entire wagon (with its standard side walls and top opening) to discharge coal. It is the track hopper system that requires bottom-discharge wagons (BOBR/BOXN-HL type) that have doors in the floor.
Q26 · True / False
If belt tension is too high (above the required maximum), the conveyor performs better and carries more coal per hour.
✅ Answer: FALSEExcessive belt tension is harmful. It overloads the belt (increasing stress and reducing belt life), overloads idler bearings, and puts excessive force on the structure and pulleys. The correct belt tension is a carefully calculated value — not "more is better." Too high tension can also cause the belt to stretch permanently or damage the splice joints.
Q27 · True / False
Coal dust that accumulates on the conveyor structure is mainly an aesthetic (cleanliness) problem and has no significant safety risk.
✅ Answer: FALSECoal dust accumulation is a serious safety hazard — not a cleanliness issue. Coal dust can: (1) self-heat and smoulder on hot surfaces (seized idlers, slipping belts), (2) form explosive dust clouds when disturbed in a confined space, (3) cause respiratory disease with long-term exposure. Regular housekeeping (dust removal from structure) is a mandatory safety activity in all Coal Handling Plants.
Q28 · True / False
The Emergency Pull Cord should only be activated in absolute life-threatening emergencies and should be avoided to prevent unnecessary production stops.
✅ Answer: FALSEThe EPC should be activated without hesitation whenever there is an unsafe condition — not only life-threatening ones. Early activation to stop the conveyor when a hazard is identified (entanglement risk, equipment abnormality, fire sign) is the correct and expected response. A conveyor restart after investigation takes minutes; an injury is irreversible. Personnel are never penalised for a genuine emergency stop.
Q29 · True / False
The inclination angle of a conveyor affects its carrying capacity — steeply inclined conveyors can carry more coal per hour than flat conveyors at the same belt speed.
✅ Answer: FALSESteeply inclined conveyors carry LESS coal per hour than flat conveyors at the same speed. On inclines, gravity acts against coal movement, reducing effective throughput. Coal also tends to roll back on steep belts. Maximum practical inclination for coal without cleated belts is about 15–18°. Beyond this, cleated belts or pipe conveyors are used.
Q30 · True / False
A belt conveyor fire typically starts at the motor because motors generate the most heat in the system.
✅ Answer: FALSEMost belt conveyor fires start at seized idlers or at points of belt slippage (drive pulley), not at the motor. A seized idler creates a local hotspot where the belt rubs across it. Accumulated coal dust at this point ignites from the frictional heat. The fire then spreads along the belt, not from the motor outward. Motors have thermal protection and are regularly maintained, making them less likely fire ignition sources than idlers in the field.

Q32 · Short Answer · Components
List FIVE major components of a belt conveyor. For each component, write ONE sentence explaining its function and ONE consequence if it fails.
📝 Model Answer (any 5 of the following) Belt: Carries coal from feed to discharge point. Failure → coal supply stops; belt replacement cost is significant.
Drive Pulley: Transfers motor power to the belt via friction. Failure → belt slips; coal movement stops; overheating.

Q33 · Short Answer · Mechanism
Explain in your own words why belt tension is critical to the operation of a belt conveyor. What happens if tension is too low? What happens if tension is too high?
📝 Model Answer Belt tension is critical because it determines how firmly the belt presses against the drive pulley. Without adequate tension, the drive pulley cannot generate enough friction to pull the belt — the pulley spins but the belt slips, stopping coal movement and generating dangerous heat.

Too low: Belt slips on drive pulley → no coal movement → friction heat at drive pulley → possible fire. Belt also sags between idlers, increasing resistance.

Too high: Excessive load on bearings and structure → shortened bearing life → possible structural damage. Belt may stretch permanently or splice joints may fail.

The take-up system maintains the tension automatically within the correct design range, balancing these two extremes.
Q34 · Short Answer · Hazard Awareness
You are walking on the inspection walkway of a running conveyor. Describe FOUR things you should NEVER do, and explain the injury or consequence associated with each.
📝 Model Answer 1. Never reach over or touch the moving belt: The belt surface can grab loose clothing, a hand, or a tool in milliseconds at 1.5–2.5 m/s — causing entanglement, degloving, or amputation with no chance of withdrawal.
2. Never open or remove pulley guards: The nip point between belt and pulley has crushing force of thousands of kg. Without the guard, accidental contact is instantly fatal.
3. Never stand below a transfer chute during operation: If the chute blocks and suddenly clears, or if coal falls sideways, you are in the trajectory of heavy coal lumps falling from height.
4. Never walk on coal spillage carelessly: Coal (especially fine coal when wet) is extremely slippery. A fall on a narrow elevated walkway near running machinery can be fatal.
(Also acceptable: Never ignore a seized idler, never bypass safety devices, never work alone in a transfer tower, never open electrical panels)
Q35 · Short Answer · Wagon Tippler
Compare the Rotary Tippler and the Track Hopper system in terms of: (a) how coal is unloaded, (b) the coal feed pattern to the conveyor, and (c) dust generation. Which system is more common in Indian thermal power plants and why?
📝 Model Answer (a) Unloading method: Rotary tippler physically rotates the entire wagon 150–160° on a cradle so coal falls out by gravity. Track hopper requires bottom-discharge wagons — the wagon floor opens and coal falls through grated tracks into a pit below, without the wagon rotating.

(b) Feed pattern: Rotary tippler produces a cyclic/batch pattern — heavy feed while wagon tips, then a pause during wagon changeover. Track hopper produces near-continuous feed as multiple wagons can simultaneously discharge as the rake moves over the hopper.

(c) Dust generation: Rotary tippler generates more dust — coal falls from height as the wagon inverts. Track hopper generates less dust because coal drops a shorter distance and the pit is partially enclosed.

More common in India: Rotary tippler is more common because it works with the existing standard BOX-N wagon fleet (no need for special bottom-discharge wagons), making it more versatile for the Indian railway coal supply chain.
Q36 · Scenario-Based · Failure Analysis
SCENARIO: During your first week of internship, you are standing near Conveyor BC-1. You notice: (1) the belt appears to be slightly off-centre toward the left side, (2) there is a faint burning smell, (3) you can see coal dust accumulating on the left side walkway. The conveyor is running normally and no alarms are sounding yet. What do you think is happening, and what is the correct course of action?
📝 Model Answer What is likely happening: The belt has begun to drift to the left side. The belt edge is making contact (or is very close to making contact) with the conveyor structure. The friction between the belt edge and the structure is generating the faint burning smell. Coal is spilling to the left side (hence the dust accumulation on the left walkway). The sway switch alarm may not have activated yet because drift is in the early stage.

Correct action:
1. Do NOT touch anything or attempt to correct the drift yourself.
2. Immediately inform the shift supervisor / shift-in-charge — verbally and clearly describe all three observations (belt off-centre, burning smell, spillage).
3. Do not leave the area without handing over observation to a responsible person.
4. If the burning smell intensifies or you see smoke, activate the Emergency Pull Cord immediately to stop the conveyor.
5. Never attempt to push the belt back into alignment manually while it is running.

Why this matters: These three symptoms together indicate a developing belt drift event. Without intervention, the belt edge will contact and abrade against the structure, generating enough heat to ignite accumulated coal dust — leading to a conveyor fire. Early reporting at this stage can prevent a major incident with a simple operational correction (adjustment of training idlers or belt loading).

Instrumentation & Safety Devices on the Conveyor

🧠 Mental Model

Think of instrumentation as the conveyor's nervous system — sensors spread across the full length that continuously sense abnormal conditions and either warn operators or automatically stop the machine before a small problem becomes a catastrophe.

A belt conveyor in a modern Coal Handling Plant is fitted with numerous sensors, switches, and protective devices along its entire length. These are not optional extras — they are mandatory safety and operational requirements under Indian Electricity Rules, factory safety regulations, and plant operating procedures. As an intern, you will see these devices everywhere on the conveyor. Knowing what each one does is essential.

Safety Device Reference Guide

DeviceLocation on ConveyorFunctionResponse When Activated
Emergency Pull Cord (EPC) Both sides, full length of conveyor, approximately every 30–50 m Allows any person anywhere along the conveyor to immediately stop it in an emergency by pulling the rope/wire Immediate emergency stop of conveyor drive motor. Conveyor cannot restart until EPC is reset and root cause investigated.
Belt Sway Switch (BSW) Both sides, spaced every 50–75 m along carry and return strand Detects lateral drift of the belt beyond a set limit. Has two positions: first position = alarm; second position = trip. First activation: Alarm in control room. Second (greater drift): Conveyor trip. Prevents belt edge from contacting steel structure.
Zero Speed Switch (ZSS) Tail pulley shaft or on a return idler Monitors belt speed. Detects if the belt has stopped (zero speed) while the motor is still running — indicating belt slip. Conveyor trip + alarm. Prevents drive pulley from running against a stationary belt (which causes fire from friction).
Belt Weigher (BW) Fixed location on the conveyor, typically mid-span Measures the weight of coal on the belt per unit length using load cells. Calculates instantaneous TPH and cumulative tonnes. Data sent to control room / DCS. No trip — provides operational data. High load may trigger an alarm.
Chute Blocked Detector (CBD) Inside transfer chutes at transfer points Detects when coal has accumulated and blocked the chute (using vibration probe, ultrasonic, or paddle switch). Alarm in control room. May automatically stop upstream feeder to prevent further coal accumulation.
Thermal / Fire Detector Inside conveyor galleries (tunnels) and transfer towers Detects elevated temperature indicating a developing fire. Linear heat detection cable runs along the belt gallery roof. Fire alarm. Conveyor trip. Activation of fire suppression system (CO₂ or water mist). Control room alert.
Underspeed Switch Drive shaft area Detects if belt is running slower than designed speed (but not zero). Indicates overloading or drive slip developing. Alarm → trip if underspeed persists. Allows early intervention before full slippage occurs.
Motor Overload Relay (OLR) Motor control centre (MCC) / switchgear panel Monitors motor current. If the motor draws current above the rated limit for a set time (indicating overload), it trips. Motor trip + alarm. Conveyor stops. Investigation required to identify overload cause before restart.
✅ What This Means For You as an Intern

When you walk a conveyor on site, you will see red ropes, yellow boxes, and small switches mounted on the frame at regular intervals — these are all part of this safety system. You will hear terms like "BSW trip," "ZSS alarm," or "EPC activation" on the radio constantly. Now you know exactly what each term means, what triggered it, and what the plant team needs to do in response.

🔴 Critical Rule – Never Bypass or Defeat Safety Devices

On some plants, under production pressure, well-meaning but incorrect actions include bypassing a belt sway switch that keeps tripping, disabling a chute blocked detector that gives "false" alarms, or tying off an EPC that was accidentally pulled. This is an extremely dangerous practice. Every safety device bypass removes one layer of protection from the system. Multiple bypasses create a condition where a fatal accident can occur without any warning. As an intern, if you are ever asked to bypass a safety device, immediately consult your supervisor. Document it. Never accept it as routine.

Step-by-Step: What to Look For at Each Location

LocationWhat a Healthy System Looks LikeWarning Signs to Report
Feed Point (Tail End) Coal lands centrally on the belt. Skirt boards are close to the belt surface with no visible gap. No coal spillage outside the belt edges. Dust suppression sprays (if fitted) are operating. Coal spilling over belt edges. Gap between skirt board and belt. Coal piling up at sides. Dust cloud larger than usual. Belt Surface (While Walking Alongside) Belt is centred on idlers. Surface is intact — no cuts, gouges, or longitudinal slits. Coal is uniformly distributed across the belt width. Belt moves smoothly without lateral wandering. Belt visibly off-centre (drift). Belt edge near or touching structure. Cuts or rips on belt surface. Uneven coal load (all on one side). Belt flapping or vibrating excessively.
Idlers (Along the Full Length) All idlers are spinning freely as the belt passes over them. No noise from any individual idler. No flat spot on any roller (from seizure). All idlers are in their correct mounting position. Any idler NOT spinning (seized — belt slides over it creating heat). Grinding or squealing noise from an idler bearing. Idler shell worn flat on one spot. Idler hanging loose or displaced from its bracket.
Structure & Walkway (Below the Belt) Walkway is clean — minimal coal spillage on the floor. Structure is intact. No coal accumulated on structural members. Return strand is clean (minimal carryback coal on the underside). Coal accumulated on structural members (fire risk). Excessive carryback falling onto the floor below. Wet coal mix on walkway (slip hazard). Smell of burning rubber or coal smoke.
Drive / Head End Coal discharges cleanly over the head pulley into the chute. Belt scraper is in contact with the belt and removing carryback. Head pulley guard is in place. No burning smell. Motor noise is normal (steady hum). Coal not discharging cleanly (flying off sideways). Burning rubber smell (belt slipping on pulley). Unusual motor noise (overload — grinding or laboured hum). Scraper not in contact with belt. Any guard missing.
Safety Devices All EPC ropes are present, unbroken, and at the correct tension (not sagging to ground, not over-tight). Belt sway switches are mounted and free. No safety device is taped up, bypassed, or disabled. EPC rope broken, missing, or sagging. Sway switch blocked with coal or debris. Any safety device with a bypass tag or wire tied in bypassed position. Missing nip point guards at pulleys.
Transfer Tower (from outside / entrance) Coal flow sound is continuous and steady from inside (not intermittent). No coal spillage on the outside of the tower base. Dust seal curtains at openings are intact. Silence from chute (blockage). Coal spilling from tower base. Dust escaping from every gap (excess spillage inside). Unusual vibration of the tower structure.
👁 Intern's Most Valuable Contribution On Site

As an intern, you will not be allowed to operate equipment. But you can walk the conveyors during your inspection rounds with a supervisor, and you can see things that busy operators may have overlooked. A fresh pair of eyes — trained by this course to know what is normal and what is not — can catch an early warning sign that prevents a major failure. Always report what you observe to your supervisor, and never assume "someone else must have seen it already."

Behavioural Guide

Do's and Don'ts for the Intern in the Coal Handling Plant

This section translates your theoretical learning into specific behaviours. These are not rules imposed on you — they are the behaviours that keep you alive and healthy during your internship and throughout your career in the power industry.

✅ DO – Always

  • ✔Always wear your complete PPE before entering the CHP area — helmet, boots, high-vis vest, and dust mask at minimum.
  • ✔Always stay on designated walkways. Never take shortcuts across structural members or below return strands.
  • ✔Always walk in pairs (buddy system) inside CHP, especially in galleries and near transfer towers.
  • ✔Always report abnormalities (seized idler, missing guard, damaged EPC) to your supervisor immediately — verbally and in writing.
  • ✔Always ask for a proper explanation before approaching any equipment you have not previously been briefed on.
  • ✔Always maintain a minimum clearance of 1 metre from the belt and moving machinery unless you are following a supervised inspection protocol.
  • ✔Always wear close-fitting clothing. Tuck in shirts. No loose ties, scarves, or dangling lanyards.
  • ✔Always know the location of the nearest Emergency Pull Cord and fire extinguisher on any conveyor you are walking.
  • ✔Always pull the EPC without hesitation if you see an entanglement, smoke, or fire — never wait for confirmation.

⛔ DON'T – Never

  • ✘Never touch a moving belt — not to feel the speed, test the surface, or remove a piece of coal from it.
  • ✘Never lean over a running conveyor for any reason — not to look at the other side, not to pick up a fallen object.
  • ✘Never attempt to clear a blocked chute or dislodged coal from a running conveyor using your hands, feet, or tools.
  • ✘Never remove guards from pulleys or other rotating parts — even "just to check" something.
  • ✘Never silence or bypass a safety alarm without a supervisor's explicit authorisation and a proper investigation.
  • ✘Never enter a transfer tower, conveyor gallery, or any confined space without a valid Confined Space Entry Permit.
  • ✘Never operate any switch, valve, or control on the conveyor unless specifically instructed and supervised by an authorised engineer.
  • ✘Never work alone in the CHP — especially in conveyor galleries, transfer towers, and tunnels below trestle conveyors.
  • ✘Never assume a stopped conveyor is safe without confirming it is Locked Out / Tagged Out (LOTO) by a qualified person.
🔴 The LOTO Principle – Stopped Does NOT Mean Safe

One of the most common causes of serious injury in industrial plants is working on equipment that appears to be stopped but is not properly isolated. A conveyor belt that stopped due to an alarm can be restarted from the control room while someone is working on it. The Lockout-Tagout (LOTO) procedure requires the drive to be electrically isolated at the switchgear, locked with a physical padlock, and tagged with the worker's identity before any maintenance work begins. As an intern, you must never approach any equipment for maintenance purposes. If asked to, immediately request that proper LOTO isolation is carried out and confirmed before you go near the equipment.

Glossary of CHP & Belt Conveyor Terms

On your first day on site you will hear these terms constantly. This glossary ensures you are not lost in the conversation.

TermMeaning
BeltThe continuous rubber-fabric loop that carries coal from tail to head end.
Belt Conveyor (BC)The complete conveyor assembly — numbered in sequence (BC-1, BC-2, etc.) in the plant.
Carry StrandThe top run of the belt that carries coal toward the discharge point.
Return StrandThe lower run of the empty belt going back from head to tail.
Head PulleyThe drive pulley at the discharge end of the conveyor.
Tail PulleyThe non-driven pulley at the loading end.
IdlerA free-spinning roller that supports the belt. Troughing idlers (carry) and flat idlers (return).
Trough AngleThe angle at which wing idler rolls are tilted to form the trough (20°–45°).
Take-upThe tensioning device — gravity, screw, or hydraulic — that keeps the belt at correct tension.
LaggingRubber coating on the drive pulley surface to increase friction with the belt.
Skirt BoardSide rubber/steel walls at the feed point that contain coal on the belt edge.
Scraper / CleanerA blade pressed against the belt at the head end to remove carryback coal.
CarrybackFine coal that sticks to the belt after discharge and is carried back on the return strand.
Transfer PointLocation where coal moves from one conveyor to the next via a chute.
ChuteThe steel guide structure that directs coal from one conveyor to the next at transfer points.
Tripper CarA movable discharge device on a conveyor above the bunker that distributes coal across bunker bays.
TermMeaning
BunkerLarge coal storage silo above the boiler, fed by the conveyor. Buffer for continuous boiler feeding.
HopperA funnel-shaped collection vessel below the wagon tippler that collects unloaded coal.
FeederA device (vibrating, apron, or belt type) that controls the rate of coal feeding onto the conveyor.
TPHTonnes Per Hour — the standard unit of conveyor throughput capacity.
CHPCoal Handling Plant — the entire system for receiving, processing, and conveying coal in a power plant.
DCSDistributed Control System — the central computerised control system monitoring all CHP parameters.
MCCMotor Control Centre — the electrical switchgear panel that starts, stops, and protects conveyor motors.
EPCEmergency Pull Cord — the safety rope running the full length of the conveyor for emergency stop.
BSWBelt Sway Switch — sensor that detects lateral belt drift and triggers alarm/trip.
ZSS / USSZero Speed Switch / Underspeed Switch — detects belt slippage or stall.
LOTOLockout-Tagout — energy isolation procedure before maintenance on conveyor or any equipment.
Nip PointThe point where the belt meets a pulley — the most dangerous entanglement zone.
Belt DriftLateral wandering of the belt off centre — can cause edge damage and fire.
Tight SideThe belt strand leaving the drive pulley (carry side) — where belt tension is highest.
Slack SideThe belt strand returning to the drive pulley — where tension is lower.
CWPCoal Workers' Pneumoconiosis — lung disease caused by long-term coal dust inhalation ("black lung").
