The Track Hopper Feeder — A Quick Refresh

You learned in L1 that coal discharged from the Wagon Tippler falls into the Underground Hopper. The Track Hopper Feeder is the device that meters coal from the bottom of that hopper onto the downstream belt conveyor at a controlled, steady rate.

There are two main types you will encounter in Indian thermal power plants:

Fig. 1 — The Feeder is the metering point between the hopper buffer and the conveyor. Disruption here propagates immediately downstream.

Two Feeder Types You Will Encounter

Vibratory Feeder

Uses electromagnetic or mechanical vibration to move coal along a trough. Simpler design, lower maintenance, well-suited to fine coal. Flow rate is adjusted by changing vibration amplitude or frequency.

Common at: smaller CHP installations, older plants

Apron / Belt Feeder

Uses a slow-moving steel apron or rubber belt to carry coal from the hopper outlet. Handles larger lumps and heavier material well. Flow rate controlled by feeder speed and a slide gate above.

Common at: large-capacity plants, high-throughput systems

ℹ
L2 Scope — What This Course Covers
This course covers assisted starting and stopping of the feeder, and supervised inspection of coal quality at the feeder input. You will not set feeder speeds, adjust gates, or respond to trips independently. You will observe, support, report, and learn to recognise normal vs. abnormal conditions.

Execution Block 1: Feeder Start Sequence

SKILL 1
Assist in starting the Track Hopper Feeder
Supervised execution only · Do not initiate independently
⛔
Execution Constraint — Understand This First
Starting the feeder is a load-sensitive event. The moment coal begins flowing from the feeder, a material load is placed on the downstream conveyor belt. If the belt is not running, not confirmed clear, or already at capacity, the result is spillage, belt mis-tracking, or a downstream trip. You cannot see the downstream belt from the feeder control station — your supervisor has system-wide awareness that you do not yet have.

What Happens When the Feeder Starts

Fig. 2 — Timeline of feeder start events. The critical observation window is the first 60 seconds after first coal contact with the belt.

Control Point 1 — Pre-Start System Readiness Check

CP 1.1
Downstream Belt Confirmed Running
SUPERVISION REQUIRED

The downstream belt conveyor must be confirmed running and at the correct speed before feeder start is commanded. This confirmation comes from the control room or from the supervisor who has checked belt status on the SCADA/PLC system. As a trainee, you do not have the authority or system access to confirm belt status — this is a supervisor action.

Your role: Stand at the feeder local panel area, observe the feeder visually, and listen for the supervisor's instruction to proceed. Do not touch the start pushbutton unless specifically directed.

CP 1.2
Hopper Level Visual Check
TRAINEE CAN ASSIST

Before start, the hopper should have sufficient coal to sustain initial flow. You can assist by checking the hopper level indicator (if visible locally) or reading the level gauge and reporting the reading to your supervisor. Low hopper level may cause intermittent feeding — important to note before starting.

What to report: Hopper level reading. Presence of any blockage at the hopper throat. Any unusual odours or visible smoke from the hopper area (can indicate spontaneous combustion).

CP 1.3
Feeder Area — Personnel Clearance
JOINT ACTION

Confirm that no personnel are in the feeder zone — under the feeder trough, near the discharge end, or inside the underground hopper area. Both you and your supervisor walk the immediate zone before any start command is issued. This is a procedural safety step and is never skipped regardless of time pressure.

Control Point 2 — Initial Coal Flow Observation Window

👁
Your Primary Role at This Point
The first 60 seconds after feeder start is the highest-risk observation window. You are positioned at a safe, designated observation point where you can see: (a) the feeder discharge end, (b) the coal contact point on the belt, and (c) the feeder drive motor. You observe and report. Your supervisor is monitoring SCADA and system response. Your local eyes are the complement to remote instrumentation.
  • Coal begins flowing steadily from the feeder outlet — no surging, no sudden large lumps, no gaps in flow
    OBSERVE
  • Feeder drive motor sound is smooth and consistent — no grinding, banging, or cyclical thumping
    LISTEN
  • Coal falls centrally on the belt at the transfer point — not to one side (which causes belt mis-tracking)
    OBSERVE
  • No unusual coal dust cloud beyond normal — excessive dust at startup indicates high coal velocity or faulty dust suppression
    OBSERVE
  • Feeder ampere reading (motor current) is within normal range — supervisor confirms on SCADA; high current at startup can indicate blockage or material jam
    SUPERVISOR
  • Belt load cell / weigh feeder reading is increasing normally — supervisor confirms material is being placed on belt as expected
    SUPERVISOR

Execution Block 2: Feeder Stop Sequence

SKILL 1
Assist in stopping the Track Hopper Feeder under live load conditions
Supervised execution only · Residual coal movement continues after stop command
⚠
Critical Execution Constraint
When a feeder stop command is issued, coal does not stop flowing immediately. There is residual material in the feeder trough, at the discharge lip, and in transit on the belt. This inertia effect lasts 10–30 seconds after the motor stops. If the downstream belt has already been stopped, this residual coal will pile at the transfer point — causing spillage and potential belt damage. Your supervisor manages the stop sequence timing. You observe and report.

System Behaviour During a Stop — The Inertia Window

Fig. 3 — Feeder output does not stop immediately after the stop command. Residual coal movement during the inertia window must be managed.

Planned Stop vs Emergency Stop — Know the Difference

Planned Stop
A coordinated shutdown following the defined sequence. Typically: feeder stops first → belt continues for clearing time (usually 3–5 minutes) → belt stops. The hopper throat gate may be closed before the feeder stop to limit residual flow. Your role is to observe the discharge end during the clearing run and confirm the belt is empty before it stops.
Emergency Stop
A system-initiated or operator-initiated immediate stop due to belt trip, blockage, safety issue, or fire. Feeder and belt may stop simultaneously. This leaves coal on the belt and in the feeder trough. Do not approach the feeder zone after an emergency stop until your supervisor has assessed the situation and confirmed it is safe to approach. Report what you saw immediately before the stop.

Control Point 3 — Assisted Stop Under Live Load

  • Supervisor confirms downstream belt will continue running for the clearing period after feeder stop — this is non-negotiable
    SUPERVISOR
  • You observe the feeder discharge end — watch for the coal tail-off: flow should reduce progressively, not cut off with a sudden material surge
    OBSERVE
  • Watch the transfer point on the belt — coal should spread evenly during tail-off, not pile up on one edge
    OBSERVE
  • Feeder motor sound should reduce smoothly to silence — an abnormal grinding or metallic sound during deceleration is a warning sign to report immediately
    LISTEN
  • Hopper throat gate closure (if applicable) — commanded by supervisor before or at stop to limit residual flow
    SUPERVISOR
  • After motor stops: both trainee and supervisor confirm the feeder is stationary before anyone approaches the feeder for inspection
    JOINT

Execution Block 3: Coal Inspection at Feeder Input

SKILL 2
Assist in inspecting coal quality and condition at the feeder input (hopper discharge zone)
Supervised inspection only · Not a cosmetic check — this is a protective safety control
🛑
Mindset Reset — Inspection Is a Safety Control
Coal inspection at the feeder input is frequently treated as a routine walkthrough. It is not. It is the last opportunity to detect material that will cause damage to the feeder, downstream equipment, or trigger a safety incident. Oversized lumps, tramp iron, wet/frozen coal, and burning coal have all entered feeders because an inspection was done carelessly or skipped entirely. When you are assigned to assist in inspection, treat it as a critical protective control — not a tick-box activity.

What You Are Inspecting and Why

👁 What You See
Coal size — are there oversized lumps near the hopper throat or on the feeder trough?
Visible tramp material — wood, metal, rope, bags, or rock mixed with coal
Coal colour — fresh coal is black; reddish-brown patches may indicate partial combustion
Ice or frozen coal crust near the hopper outlet (in cold weather)
Any spillage or accumulated coal outside the feeder trough path
Visible blockage or bridging at the hopper throat
👂 What You Hear
Metallic clanking from within the feeder — possible tramp iron in the material flow
Excessive banging or thumping — large lumps passing through or impact with feeder structure
Unusual grinding — coal fines packed between feeder trough and side plates
High-pitched squealing from drive motor — possible belt/chain tension issue
Rumbling from the hopper — coal avalanche inside; listen for stability
🌡 What You Sense
Smoke or burning smell — possible spontaneous combustion in the hopper. Report IMMEDIATELY — do not continue inspection
Excessive heat near the hopper wall — thermal hotspot indicator
Unusual chemical smell — contaminated coal or leaked hydraulic fluid on coal
Coal dust in the air — compare to normal; sudden increase indicates dust suppression failure
✋ What You Feel (with PPE)
Feeder trough vibration with hand on structure (never on moving parts) — abnormal vibration indicates imbalance
Heat on feeder motor casing (back of hand, never palm first) — hot to touch without a warm-up period suggests bearing issue
Excessive dust deposition on nearby surfaces — gauge relative to normal for that day
Ground vibration — large lumps passing through cause perceptible floor vibration

Coal Size Assessment — Why It Matters Here

The feeder is designed to handle coal up to a maximum lump size — typically 150mm–300mm depending on the feeder type and downstream crusher capacity. Coal that exceeds this size causes:

Blockage Risk
Oversized coal jams at the feeder discharge lip
Coal bridges across the hopper throat, stopping flow
Feeder motor stalls under excess load
Damage Risk
Lumps exceeding crusher capacity break crusher teeth
Hard foreign objects (tramp iron) damage conveyor belt and idlers
Impact damage to feeder pan / trough structure
Normal Appearance
Mixed coal with majority under 150mm
No visible metallic material or non-coal items
Uniform black colour, moist but not saturated
No visible smoke, flame, or burning odour
⚙
Tramp Iron — A Special Hazard
Tramp iron refers to metal objects — wire rope, wagon parts, bolts, old tools — that have entered the coal stream and are not detectable by visual inspection of surface coal alone. Most coal handling plants have a Magnetic Separator and a Metal Detector on the conveyor downstream of the feeder. Your inspection reduces the gross hazard; the instrumentation catches what you cannot. If you see obvious metal during your visual inspection, stop and report to your supervisor immediately — do not attempt to remove it yourself.

Inspection Safety Rules

Always Do
Wear full PPE — helmet, safety shoes, dust mask (N95 minimum), hearing protection, hi-vis vest
Stay on the designated inspection walkway — never stand directly over or below the feeder trough
Keep a safe distance from the moving feeder pan/trough — minimum 1 metre
Use a torch for inspecting dark areas of the hopper throat
Report every finding verbally to your supervisor — even if you think it looks "probably fine"
Record your observations in the shift log as directed by your supervisor
Never Do
Never approach a running feeder trough from the side where coal is actively discharging
Never lean over a running feeder or hopper throat to get a better look
Never attempt to remove an obstruction from a running feeder — ever
Never inspect alone — L2 inspection is always with your supervisor present
Never ignore a burning smell — treat it as an emergency until confirmed otherwise by supervisor
Never enter the underground hopper area for inspection without proper LOTO and permit

Observation Windows — Reading System Behaviour

A large part of your value as an L2 learner is your presence on the floor. Instruments are not everywhere, and local observation is a genuine layer of protection. Knowing what normal looks like is the only way to recognise abnormal.

Normal vs. Abnormal — Quick Reference

What You Are Watching Normal Condition Early Warning Act
Coal flow from feeder outlet Steady, continuous, even width Pulsing, gaps, surges REPORT
Coal landing on belt Centred, consistent pile height Displaced to one side REPORT
Feeder motor sound Smooth, consistent hum Thumping, grinding, cyclical sound REPORT IMMEDIATELY
Feeder trough vibration Even vibration (vibratory type) or smooth movement (apron type) Irregular, jerky, or hammering vibration REPORT
Dust levels at feeder Low; water mist visible from suppression Dust cloud increasing; mist absent REPORT
Coal smell Normal fresh coal odour Burning or acrid smell REPORT IMMEDIATELY
Motor casing temperature Warm to back of hand — not hot Hot — cannot hold hand near casing REPORT IMMEDIATELY
Belt tracking at transfer point Belt running centrally on idlers Belt drifting to one side REPORT
Hopper throat area Open, coal freely moving downward Coal bridging / arch forming REPORT IMMEDIATELY
✔
Your Reporting Protocol at L2
At L2, your job when you spot an early warning condition is simple: stop what you are doing, turn to your supervisor, and say what you saw, clearly and factually. Use specific language: "I can see coal falling to the right side of the belt at the feeder transfer point" is far more useful than "something looks off." You do not decide what happens next — that is the supervisor's role. Your job is to observe and report, accurately and immediately.

Common Mistakes in Assisted Execution

These are the execution errors that occur most often among trainees during their first supervised interactions with feeder systems. They are not listed to make you nervous — they are listed so you recognise the pattern if you start heading toward it.

Mistake Why It Happens Consequence Severity
Touching the start button before supervisor confirms system readiness Eagerness to help; mistaking presence of button for permission to use it Feeder starts before downstream belt is ready; spillage or belt trip HIGH
Standing in the coal discharge fall zone during observation Wanting a better viewing angle; not knowing the designated safe observation point Coal impact injury; coal dust inhalation; being caught in moving material HIGH
Treating inspection as a quick visual pass — not pausing to observe carefully Time pressure; assumption that nothing will be wrong Hazardous material (tramp iron, oversized coal) enters the system undetected HIGH
Not reporting an early warning condition because "it seems minor" Self-doubt; not wanting to raise a false alarm; not knowing what normal is Small issues become failures; early warning missed means later incident HIGH
Walking away from the feeder during the post-start observation window Feeling that once it starts, the job is done Abnormal behaviour in the first 60 seconds goes undetected MEDIUM
Not accounting for inertia during a stop — assuming coal stops when the motor stops Intuition from everyday experience (switches turn things off instantly) Misreads the discharge end as clear when coal is still flowing; wrong report MEDIUM
Attempting to remove obstruction from the feeder trough Wanting to solve the problem; not wanting to slow things down Entanglement injury; crush injury; falls into trough CRITICAL / FATAL

Failure Cases — Learn from What Went Wrong

These cases are drawn from real failure scenarios in coal handling plant environments. The details have been presented as learning cases. Read each case as if you were the trainee in that situation.

CASE 01
Assisted Start Without Coordinated Readiness — Coal Accumulated at Transfer Point
Situation
A trainee, working with a supervisor who stepped away briefly, saw the feeder start instruction in the shift log and initiated the feeder start from the local panel without waiting for supervisor return and system confirmation.
What happened
The downstream conveyor had not yet been started. Coal from the feeder fell at the transfer point and accumulated rapidly. The belt started shortly after but was loaded unevenly, causing mis-tracking. The belt drifted and rubbed against the stringer, causing damage to the belt edge.
Root cause
The trainee was focused only on the local feeder and had no visibility of downstream belt status. They interpreted "feeder start instruction in the log" as authorisation to act independently. Starting without confirmed system readiness is not assisted execution — it is unassisted execution.
Lesson
The system is coupled. The feeder does not exist alone. A start action at the feeder is a start action on the entire downstream chain simultaneously. Supervised start exists precisely to manage these couplings — the supervisor's system awareness is not a formality, it is the safety layer you do not yet have.
CASE 02
Inadequate Supervised Inspection — Oversized Material Entered Feeder, Partial Blockage
Situation
During a pre-start inspection of the feeder input zone, the trainee and supervisor walked the area quickly. The trainee noted the general coal appearance as "normal" without looking into the hopper throat zone carefully. A large lump (~400mm) was wedged at the hopper throat, partially visible above the coal surface.
What happened
The feeder started normally. As coal drained from above the lump, the large piece fell onto the feeder pan and jammed the apron chain. The feeder motor stalled and tripped. Clearing the blockage took 2.5 hours, with a permitted entry into the hopper area under LOTO. The plant ran on reduced coal stock during the outage.
Root cause
Inspection was done standing and walking — it was not a careful, systematic look. The hopper throat area requires a deliberate pause and a close look with a torch if there is any doubt. The trainee assumed surface-level observation was sufficient.
Lesson
Assisted inspection is a protective control. The purpose is to detect what the instruments cannot and what the surface appearance hides. A proper inspection takes 3–5 minutes minimum, uses a torch in dark zones, and reports any uncertainty immediately — not after deciding it is probably fine. If you are unsure what you are looking at, say so. That is the correct answer.
CASE 03
Improper Assisted Stop Under Live Load — Material Buildup Caused Belt Stress and Mis-Tracking
Situation
During a planned system shutdown, the trainee was asked to monitor the feeder discharge during the stop sequence. The trainee observed the feeder motor stop and reported to the supervisor that "feeder is stopped." The supervisor, relying on this confirmation, initiated belt stop sooner than the clearing time required.
What happened
The motor had stopped, but coal was still discharging from the residual material in the trough. When the belt stopped, this residual coal piled at the transfer point. The off-centre pile caused belt mis-tracking on restart, and the belt required re-training by a maintenance crew — adding 1.5 hours to restart time.
Root cause
The trainee did not understand that "motor stopped" and "coal flow stopped" are not simultaneous events. The report of "feeder is stopped" was factually correct about the motor, but the intended meaning — that coal discharge had ceased — was not yet true. Imprecise reporting led to a premature belt stop decision.
Lesson
Report what you see, not what you infer. "Motor has stopped" and "coal is still flowing from the discharge end" are two separate, simultaneous facts — report both. Your supervisor needs the complete picture. The inertia window is 10–30 seconds of critical post-motor-stop observation — do not leave the discharge point until flow has actually stopped.

Your Role Boundary — What L2 Means in Practice

📋
The Definition of Assisted Execution
Assisted execution means you act on instruction, observe actively, and report accurately. You do not decide to start, stop, or adjust anything independently. You do not diagnose faults. You do not escalate to anyone other than your immediate supervisor. Your value at this stage is your attentiveness, your communication, and your willingness to report what you see — even when you are not sure it matters.
Within Your L2 Boundary
Observe and report feeder discharge behaviour during start and stop
Report hopper level reading to supervisor when asked
Confirm the feeder area is clear of personnel before start (as part of joint check)
Perform visual and sensory inspection of coal at feeder input alongside supervisor
Record observations in the shift log as directed by supervisor
Operate a local start/stop button when specifically instructed by supervisor who is present
Report any abnormal condition immediately to the supervisor, clearly and factually
Outside Your L2 Boundary
Do not initiate any feeder start or stop without an explicit instruction from your present supervisor
Do not adjust feeder speed or gate position
Do not override, bypass, or reset any interlock or trip
Do not diagnose a fault and take action on your own conclusion
Do not approach a running feeder trough for any reason without supervisor clearance
Do not remove obstructions from feeder, trough, or hopper throat
Do not enter the underground hopper area at any time during this course level
Do not communicate a system problem directly to the control room without going through your supervisor first
🧑‍🏭
Final Word from Your Trainer
You will be ready for L3 — independent execution — when two things are true: you have completed enough supervised cycles that you can predict what the system will do before it does it, and your supervisor trusts your observation and reporting enough to give you room. That trust is built one honest, careful, attentive round at a time. Don't rush it. The system is patient. The hazards are not.

What You Should Be Ready to Do — Under Supervision

Recognise that feeder start is a system-wide load event, not a local action
Observe and report the first 60 seconds of coal flow after feeder start
Identify the inertia window after feeder stop and observe discharge tail-off
Distinguish between planned stop and emergency stop, and behave accordingly
Perform a systematic 4-sense inspection of coal at the feeder input
Identify normal vs. early warning conditions at the feeder and transfer point
Report findings specifically and factually — describe what you see, not what you infer
Recognise tramp iron, oversized coal, bridging, and spontaneous combustion signs
State your role boundary clearly — know what is and is not within your authority
Understand all three failure cases and the lesson each carries
